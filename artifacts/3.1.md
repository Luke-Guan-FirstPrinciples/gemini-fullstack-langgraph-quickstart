# **The Global Architecture of Quantum Technology Standardization: A Comprehensive Analysis (Pre-2023)**

## **1\. Introduction to the Quantum Standardization Imperative**

The transition of quantum technologies from the domain of theoretical physics to the realm of applied engineering represents one of the most profound paradigm shifts in the history of modern computing and global telecommunications. As quantum computing, quantum communication, and quantum sensing edge steadily toward commercial viability, the global technology ecosystem faces a critical and highly complex bottleneck: the historical absence of universally accepted, rigorously defined technical standards. Without comprehensive standardization, the emergent quantum industry risks severe market fragmentation, insurmountable vendor lock-in, and a fundamental inability to integrate novel quantum hardware seamlessly with existing classical digital infrastructure.

Furthermore, the theoretical advent of cryptographically relevant quantum computers (CRQCs)—machines capable of executing Shor’s algorithm to factor large prime numbers and compute discrete logarithms—poses an existential threat to the asymmetric cryptographic algorithms (such as RSA and Elliptic Curve Cryptography) that currently secure the global internet. This looming cryptographic crisis has precipitated an urgent, coordinated mandate across international boundaries to develop, test, and standardize quantum-safe security mechanisms. The industry response to this threat is broadly categorized into two distinct technological pathways: algorithmic defenses utilizing mathematical complexity, known as Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC), and hardware-based physical defenses leveraging the immutable laws of quantum mechanics, known as Quantum Key Distribution (QKD). Both domains require immense standardization efforts to ensure global interoperability and absolute security verification.

This report provides an exhaustive, highly detailed analysis of the global quantum technology standardization landscape, capturing the precise state of the industry up to the end of December 2022\. It focuses specifically on the foundational and highly active standardization bodies driving this nascent domain: the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI), the International Telecommunication Union Telecommunication Standardization Sector (ITU-T), the International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission (ISO/IEC JTC 1), the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), and the Internet Research Task Force (IRTF). By examining the specific working groups responsible for quantum work, their core technical areas of focus, and the underlying mechanisms of their draft and published documents, this report extracts deep second- and third-order insights into the strategic trajectories of these organizations. The resulting synthesis reveals a complex, highly coordinated, yet occasionally overlapping global effort to lay the infrastructural, linguistic, and security foundations for the next generation of global telecommunication networks.

## **2\. European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI)**

The European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI) has functioned as a pioneering force in the realm of quantum technology standardization, largely driven by its early and acute recognition of the security implications that quantum mechanics pose to classical telecommunication infrastructures. Rather than treating quantum technology as a monolithic entity, ETSI’s strategic approach is distinctly bifurcated into two parallel, yet ultimately complementary, tracks. The first track focuses on hardware-based, physical-layer security through the implementation of Quantum Key Distribution (QKD), while the second track focuses on algorithmic, mathematical security through the deployment of Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC).1

## **2.1. Industry Specification Group on Quantum Key Distribution (ISG QKD)**

Established in 2008, the Industry Specification Group on Quantum Key Distribution (ISG QKD) is the telecommunication industry's longest-standing dedicated forum for the standardization of quantum communication technologies.2 Originally founded by European members, the ISG QKD had, by the end of 2022, grown into a massive global consortium comprising major multinational telecommunication operators, national metrology institutes (NMIs), system integrators, academic laboratories, and representatives from North America and Asia.1

The primary, overarching focus of the ISG QKD is to facilitate the commercial deployment and market stimulation of QKD systems. It achieves this by defining standardized system interfaces, outlining rigorous implementation security requirements, characterizing the complex optical components of QKD transmitters and receivers, and establishing highly specific control interfaces for Software-Defined Networks (SDN).1 QKD technology utilizes the fundamental, subatomic principles of quantum mechanics—specifically Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle and the no-cloning theorem—to distribute symmetric cryptographic keys with mathematically proven information-theoretic security. However, while the mathematical protocols underlying QKD (such as the BB84 protocol) are unconditionally secure in theory, the physical implementations of these protocols are vulnerable to side-channel attacks, such as Trojan-horse attacks on optical components or detector blinding attacks. The ISG QKD's core mandate is to bridge this treacherous gap between theoretical quantum security and practical, deployable, secure hardware.1

By the end of 2022, the ISG QKD had published a highly mature, heavily referenced portfolio of Group Specifications (GS) and Group Reports (GR). The following table outlines the key standards relevant to the commercialization and integration of QKD networks:

| Document Number | Title | Focus Area and Technical Context |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **ETSI GS QKD 004** | Application Interface | Specifies the foundational REST Application Programming Interface (API) that allows classical software applications to securely request and receive symmetric cryptographic keys from an underlying QKD network, abstracting the quantum physics away from the application developer.4 |
| **ETSI GR QKD 007** | Vocabulary | Consolidates, defines, and standardizes abbreviations and terminology, harmonizing the complex lexicon derived from both quantum physics and classical cryptography to prevent semantic fragmentation across the industry.4 |
| **ETSI GS QKD 012** | Device and Communication Channel Parameters for QKD Deployment | Details the strict physical and operational optical parameters (such as attenuation, dark count rates, and chromatic dispersion) required for deploying QKD over existing, shared optical fiber telecommunication networks.4 |
| **ETSI GS QKD 014** | REST-based Key Delivery API | A significant revision and modernization of the application interface, specifying a highly scalable REST-based API for delivering keys to multi-tenant applications in cloud environments.4 |
| **ETSI GS QKD 015** | Control Interface for Software Defined Networks | Specifies the highly critical interface connecting a physical QKD system to an overarching SDN controller, enabling the dynamic routing, provisioning, and self-healing of quantum channels across complex optical topologies.5 |
| **ETSI GS QKD 016** | Common Criteria Protection Profile \- Pair of Prepare and Measure QKD Modules | Defines rigid security functional and assurance requirements based on the internationally recognized Common Criteria (ISO/IEC 15408\) framework, specifically tailored for Prepare-and-Measure QKD implementations.5 |

The development and publication of ETSI GS QKD 015 and ETSI GS QKD 018 represent a profound, structural shift in QKD deployment philosophy. Early QKD systems were rigid, point-to-point optical links that required dedicated dark fiber and manual configuration. By aggressively standardizing SDN control interfaces and orchestration layers, ETSI has paved the way for dynamic, software-controlled quantum networks. In these modernized networks, the routing of quantum keys can be automatically optimized based on real-time network load, fiber attenuation fluctuations, and live threat detection.1 This transition from static links to dynamic grids is the critical enabler for integrating quantum security into agile, cloud-native 5G and 6G telecommunication backbones.

## **2.2. Technical Committee Cyber Security (TC CYBER) and Quantum-Safe Cryptography (QSC)**

While the ISG QKD focuses intensively on physical optical hardware, ETSI’s Technical Committee Cyber Security (TC CYBER) addresses the software, protocol, and algorithmic defenses against quantum computing through its dedicated Quantum-Safe Cryptography (QSC) working group.2 The QSC group rigorously evaluates the impact of quantum computing on existing symmetric and asymmetric cryptographic algorithms and provides highly practical, deployable recommendations for industry migration to quantum-resistant primitives.2

It is highly notable that TC CYBER QSC does not invent or standardize novel cryptographic mathematical algorithms—that fundamental mathematical responsibility falls to national bodies such as the U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST). Instead, TC CYBER operates at a higher level of abstraction, focusing on the complex architectural and protocol-level integration of these newly standardized algorithms into existing legacy networks and internet protocols.2 As of late 2022, the group's primary areas of focus centered on hybrid key exchange mechanisms, quantum-safe digital signatures, Virtual Private Networks (VPNs), identity-based encryption, and the creation of practical, phased migration frameworks.6

Key technical reports and specifications published by TC CYBER QSC up to the end of 2022 include:

| Document Number | Title | Focus Area and Technical Context |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **ETSI TS 103 744** | Quantum-Safe Hybrid Key Exchanges | Specifies critical technical methods for deriving composite cryptographic keys from multiple shared secrets. This allows for combining classical algorithms (like Diffie-Hellman) with newly proposed PQC algorithms or QKD systems to hedge against unforeseen algorithmic vulnerabilities.12 |
| **ETSI TR 103 570** | Quantum-Safe Key Exchanges | Analyzes and categorizes different architectural approaches and integration strategies for embedding quantum-safe primitives into universally used key exchange protocols like TLS and IKEv2.13 |
| **ETSI TR 103 616** | Quantum-Safe Signatures | Provides deep technical descriptions of post-quantum digital signature schemes, rigorously analyzing their execution performance, massive public key sizes, and the resulting integration challenges within existing Public Key Infrastructures (PKI) and certificate authorities.6 |
| **ETSI TR 103 617** | Quantum-Safe Virtual Private Networks (VPN) | Evaluates the complex integration of quantum-safe cryptography into IPsec and TLS-based VPN architectures, addressing packet fragmentation issues caused by large PQC ciphertexts.13 |
| **ETSI TR 103 618** | Quantum-Safe Identity-Based Encryption | Explores the mathematical feasibility and network utility of identity-based encryption (IBE) schemes that remain theoretically resistant to quantum attacks.13 |

## **2.3. Second-Order Insights on ETSI’s Standardization Strategy**

An in-depth analysis of ETSI’s pre-2023 standardization output reveals a highly pragmatic, risk-averse, and commercially driven standardization philosophy. The aggressive push for "Quantum-Safe Hybrid Key Exchanges" (codified in ETSI TS 103 744\) indicates a strong industry consensus that relying solely on newly minted PQC mathematical algorithms carries immense inherent risks. History dictates that mathematical proofs of security can occasionally be overturned by subsequent classical cryptographic breakthroughs. By standardizing hybrid protocols, ETSI enables organizations to overlay PQC or QKD on top of classical, battle-tested elliptic curve cryptography. This creates a robust defense-in-depth architecture, ensuring that the telecommunication system remains secure as long as at least one of the underlying algorithms (classical or quantum) remains unbroken.3

Furthermore, the dual, parallel focus on both QKD (hardware solutions) and QSC (software solutions) allows ETSI to serve entirely distinct market segments simultaneously. QSC is positioned for widespread, low-cost, algorithmic upgrades to general internet applications, whereas QKD is positioned for high-assurance, capital-intensive critical infrastructure protection, such as inter-bank clearing networks and government communication trunk lines.

## **3\. International Telecommunication Union \- Telecommunication Standardization Sector (ITU-T)**

If ETSI operates as the agile, security-focused pioneer of quantum communication standards, the ITU-T functions as the overarching global architect for massive-scale, carrier-grade network integration. The ITU-T approaches quantum technologies strictly from the perspective of national telecommunications monopolies, tier-one backbone operators, and massive-scale global service providers.18 Up to the end of 2022, the ITU-T was arguably the most prolific and structurally comprehensive publisher of quantum network standards, distributing a massive workload across a dedicated pre-standardization Focus Group and multiple highly specialized, permanent Study Groups.20

## **3.1. Focus Group on Quantum Information Technology for Networks (FG-QIT4N)**

Established in September 2019 and formally concluding its mandate in December 2021, the FG-QIT4N served as a vital pre-standardization incubator and collaborative think-tank.2 The group provided an open platform for global stakeholders to study both near-term Quantum Key Distribution Networks (QKDN) and broader, highly theoretical Quantum Information Networks (QIN).2 Its primary role was to scan the technological horizon, establish baseline universally agreed-upon terminologies, identify commercial use cases, and generate comprehensive research material that would subsequently feed directly into the normative, binding work of the permanent ITU-T Study Groups.19

In November 2021, culminating its efforts, the FG-QIT4N adopted nine foundational, heavily researched Technical Reports 19:

| Document Number | Title | Focus Area and Analytical Context |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **FG QIT4N D1.1** | QIT4N terminology: Network aspects of QITs | Establishes general quantum information technology terminology, creating a lexicon for overarching quantum network design.20 |
| **FG QIT4N D1.2** | QIT4N use cases: Network aspects of QITs | Details future application scenarios for general QIT, such as distributed quantum computing and blind quantum computation.19 |
| **FG QIT4N D1.4** | Standardization outlook and technology maturity: Network aspects of QITs | Provides a rigorous gap analysis and maturity assessment for general QIN technologies, explicitly identifying areas requiring fundamental physics breakthroughs.6 |
| **FG QIT4N D2.1** | QIT4N terminology: QKDN | Formulates definitions specifically and exclusively targeting deployable QKD networks.6 |
| **FG QIT4N D2.2** | QIT4N use cases: QKDN | Outlines immediate commercial and operational use cases for QKD, such as securing 5G fronthaul and financial datacenter interconnects.6 |
| **FG QIT4N D2.3** | QKDN protocols: Quantum layer | Analyzes the fundamental protocols required for the physical optical transport and measurement of raw qubits over fiber.19 |
| **FG QIT4N D2.3** | QKDN protocols: Key management layer, control layer and management layer | Analyzes the classical networking layers required to distil, store, and route the symmetric keys generated by the quantum layer.19 |
| **FG QIT4N D2.4** | QKDN transport technologies | Evaluates the classical transport mechanisms required to carry quantum metadata alongside actual telecommunication traffic.19 |
| **FG QIT4N D2.5** | Standardization outlook and technology maturity: QKDN | Provides a definitive assessment of QKDN readiness, concluding that the technology is mature enough for immediate normative standardization.4 |

The deliberate and highly structured separation of general "QITs" (the D1 series) from specific "QKDN" (the D2 series) highlights an important technological bifurcation recognized by the ITU-T. QKDN is acknowledged as commercially available, operationally mature, and ready for immediate normative standardization. Conversely, broader QIT applications (such as entanglement-based quantum repeaters and a true quantum internet) are explicitly recognized as highly immature, requiring longer-term academic study before normative standardization can occur.3

Following the exploratory baseline work of FG-QIT4N, ITU-T Study Group 13 (SG13), which holds the global mandate for overseeing Future Networks, absorbed the responsibility to define the overarching functional architecture of Quantum Key Distribution Networks.18 SG13 treats QKDN not as a standalone, isolated cryptographic tool, but as a robust, distinct, layered network infrastructure that must interoperate seamlessly with, and overlay upon, existing classical carrier networks.25

By the close of 2022, SG13 had finalized and published a comprehensive suite of Recommendations under the **Y.3800 sub-series**, establishing a definitive, globally recognized blueprint for carrier-grade QKD deployment 21:

In this model, quantum keys are provisioned dynamically to enterprise clients with guaranteed Service Level Agreements (SLAs) over complex metropolitan or wide-area network fiber topologies.

## **3.3. Study Group 17: Security Aspects of QKDN**

While SG13 designs the functional network architecture, Study Group 17 (SG17) operates as the lead group for overarching cybersecurity within the ITU-T. SG17's specific mandate regarding quantum technologies involves comprehensively analyzing theoretical threat vectors, defining rigid security frameworks, and specifying the exact cryptographic integration techniques for QKDN.30 Their normative outputs, categorized under the **X.1700 sub-series**, are essential for assuring telecommunication providers that the integration of novel QKD hardware into classical networks does not inadvertently introduce new vulnerabilities or bridge secure air-gaps.30

By the end of 2022, SG17 had published or substantially progressed several critical Recommendations:

| Document Number | Title | Technical Scope and Security Context |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **ITU-T X.1710** | Security framework for quantum key distribution networks | Provides the foundational, comprehensive threat analysis and establishes the overall security requirements for all interacting QKDN components.4 |
| **ITU-T X.1712** | Security requirements and measures for QKDN – Key management | Specifies stringent security controls exclusively for the Key Management Agent (KMA), mandating secure storage environments, strict access controls, and highly secure key synchronization processes across nodes.4 |
| **ITU-T X.1714** | Key combination and confidential key supply for QKDN | Defines the complex mathematical and procedural mechanisms for combining physically derived QKD keys with other key exchange methods (e.g., Diffie-Hellman or PQC) and supplying them securely to end-user applications without leakage.4 |
| **ITU-T X.1715** | Security requirements and measures for integration of QKDN and secure storage network | Provides highly specific threat analyses and mitigations for environments linking QKD to secure mass storage systems, including critical Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) integration considerations.4 |

Furthermore, by late 2022, SG17 was actively developing draft documents such as **X.1716** (Authentication and authorization in QKDN) and **X.1717** (Security requirements and measures for QKDN control and management).36 The focus on X.1714 is particularly noteworthy and provides a crucial third-order insight. It reflects the exact same pragmatic, defense-in-depth risk-management philosophy observed in ETSI’s hybrid key exchange standards. By explicitly standardizing the methods to combine QKD keys with classical algorithms, SG17 ensures a strategy that satisfies legacy government compliance requirements (which often mandate classical algorithms) while simultaneously providing physical-layer quantum resistance against future attacks.12

By the end of 2022, SG11 was actively developing the **Q.4100 sub-series** of protocols, effectively mapping the theoretical architecture to actionable network code 39:

## **3.5. Systemic Implications of ITU-T's Carrier-Grade Focus**

The synergistic relationship between SG13 (Architecture), SG17 (Security), and SG11 (Signalling) demonstrates an exceptionally high level of strategic, long-term coordination. A profound third-order insight derived from the ITU-T’s massive standardization pattern is the clear intent to fully commoditize and modularize quantum encryption hardware. By defining standard interfaces down to the signalling level (Ak, Kx, Ck), the ITU-T ensures that a global telecommunications provider can purchase a quantum optical transmitter from Vendor A, a classical key management server from Vendor B, and a cloud-based SDN controller from Vendor C, and integrate them seamlessly into an existing classical network. This strict modular interoperability is the absolute critical prerequisite for breaking early vendor monopolies, preventing lock-in, and driving down the immense capital expenditure (CapEx) currently associated with deploying quantum infrastructure at a national scale.

## **4\. International Organization for Standardization / International Electrotechnical Commission (ISO/IEC JTC 1\)**

While the ITU-T focuses heavily on telecommunications architecture and wide-area networking, the Joint Technical Committee 1 (JTC 1\) of the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) operates at a much more foundational, horizontal level. JTC 1 deals with core information technology concepts, base ontologies, and rigorous, legally binding security evaluations.41 The establishment of quantum-specific working groups within JTC 1 reflects the deep, systemic integration of quantum mechanics into classical computer science and formal systems engineering disciplines.

## **4.1. Working Group 14 (WG 14): Quantum Computing and Systems Integration**

Recognizing the rapid, heavily capitalized advancement of quantum processors globally, JTC 1 established Working Group 14 (WG 14\) on Quantum Computing in June 2020\.41 WG 14 was designed to function as a horizontal systems integration entity, tasked with comprehensively mapping the standardization landscape, identifying critical technological gaps, and maintaining formal relationships with other ISO/IEC subcommittees to prevent duplicative work.43

In November 2022, during the JTC 1 Tokyo Plenary, recognizing the expanding scope of the field, WG 14 was officially renamed to "Quantum Information Technology," and its terms of reference were deliberately expanded to cover a broader scope encompassing quantum simulation and fundamental quantum systems.46 Up to the end of 2022, WG 14’s primary, highly critical focus was establishing foundational semantics and vocabulary.

Key deliverables under development by WG 14 as of late 2022 include:

| Document Number | Title | Focus Area and Ontological Significance |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **ISO/IEC DIS 4879** | Information technology — Quantum computing — Terminology and vocabulary | Standardizes core definitions (such as projective measurements, quantum coherence, and entanglement) to establish a rigid, common language across diverse disciplines of software developers, theoretical physicists, and systems engineers.43 |
| **ISO/IEC TR 18157** | Study report on Quantum Computing | Serves as a continuous, living repository of evolving use cases, standardization gap analyses, and technological maturity trajectories to guide future ISO work items.44 |

The systemic importance of ISO/IEC 4879 cannot be overstated in the context of commercialization. A major historical barrier to quantum commercialization has been profound semantic divergence—physicists use entirely different terminology and mathematical representations than classical computer scientists or network engineers. By standardizing the vocabulary, WG 14 provides the essential linguistic scaffolding upon which all subsequent software, hardware, and legal standards must be built.47

## **4.2. Subcommittee 27 (SC 27\) Working Group 3: QKD Security Evaluation**

Perhaps the single most critical standardization effort for the immediate market adoption of quantum technology is housed within JTC 1/SC 27, the subcommittee responsible globally for Information Security, Cybersecurity, and Privacy Protection.49 Specifically, SC 27’s Working Group 3 (Security evaluation, testing and specification) is tasked with developing standards to adapt existing, classical security evaluation frameworks to the highly unique, fragile physics of quantum cryptography.50

The global gold standard for IT security evaluation is ISO/IEC 15408, commonly known as the Common Criteria (CC). However, the Common Criteria was designed exclusively for classical software and traditional silicon hardware, evaluating logical software flaws, buffer overflows, and classical power-draw side channels. QKD, which relies on the transmission and measurement of individual single photons, faces entirely novel, physics-based threat vectors. These include side-channel attacks targeting the avalanche photodiodes (APDs), detector blinding attacks (where an adversary shoots bright light into a detector to force it into classical mode), and precise optical timing attacks.50 Classical IT evaluation methodologies are completely blind to these quantum-optical vulnerabilities.

To address this massive evaluation gap, SC 27 WG 3 initiated the development of a two-part draft standard, **ISO/IEC 23837**, which acts as a vital bridge between the classical Common Criteria framework and the physical realities of quantum optics 51:

| Draft Document | Title | Technical Scope and Evaluation Mechanics |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **ISO/IEC DIS 23837-1** | Security requirements, test and evaluation methods for quantum key distribution — Part 1: Requirements | Specifies the formal security problem definitions, functional requirements, and strict assurance requirements specifically tailored for QKD transmitter and receiver optical modules.43 |
| **ISO/IEC DIS 23837-2** | Security requirements, test and evaluation methods for quantum key distribution — Part 2: Evaluation and testing methods | Defines the exact, rigorous laboratory testing methodologies required for independent evaluators to test physical optical components against quantum-specific vulnerabilities.43 |

The strategic and commercial impact of ISO/IEC 23837 is immense. Government, military, and defense agencies—the primary early adopters and key funders of QKD networks—legally mandate Common Criteria certification for the procurement of any cryptographic equipment.8 Without a standardized, internationally recognized testing methodology for optical components, QKD systems simply cannot be sold to these highly lucrative entities. Consequently, the development of ISO/IEC 23837 is the critical, foundational enabler for unlocking the global governmental and defense QKD market.

## **4.3. Exploratory Domains: Software Engineering (SC 7\) and Cloud Computing (SC 38\)**

Beyond the focused work of WG 14 and SC 27, JTC 1 engaged in vital exploratory studies via its other established subcommittees. Subcommittee 7 (SC 7), which focuses on Software and Systems Engineering, investigated the highly unique challenges of quantum software engineering. SC 7 acknowledged that quantum algorithms, due to their probabilistic nature and reliance on entanglement, require entirely new software lifecycle processes, specialized debugging tools, and novel validation methods that classical software engineering cannot provide.44 Concurrently, Subcommittee 38 (SC 38), focusing on Cloud Computing and Distributed Platforms, examined the integration of super-cooled quantum processors as cloud-accessed hardware accelerators (Quantum-as-a-Service). SC 38 explored how distributed cloud architectures must adapt to allocate highly scarce quantum processing resources to end-users securely over the internet.54

## **4.4. The Strategic Value of ISO/IEC’s Horizontal Standardization**

A deeper analysis of ISO/IEC's activities reveals a methodical, "bottom-up" systems engineering approach. Rather than rushing to define high-level wide-area network architectures (as seen in the ITU-T), ISO/IEC focuses on the unglamorous but absolutely vital foundations: agreeing on what scientific words mean (WG 14\) and determining exactly how to legally and physically certify that a component is secure (SC 27). This horizontal integration provides the raw, universally accepted materials that vertical standards organizations rely upon. For example, ETSI's GS QKD 016 (Common Criteria Protection Profile) directly and heavily relies on the foundational evaluation testing framework being developed by SC 27 in ISO/IEC 23837\.

## **5\. Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE)**

The IEEE approaches quantum technology standardization from a deeply technical, component-level, and software-stack engineering perspective. Utilizing the massive organizational weight of the IEEE Standards Association (IEEE SA) and heavily leveraging its extensive network of specialized societies (including the Communications Society, Computer Society, and Nanotechnology Council), the IEEE aims to make quantum computing highly accessible to a diverse array of software developers, mathematicians, and hardware engineers.56

As of late 2022, the IEEE had built a highly robust, multi-layered pipeline of eight active standards projects (the P-series) dedicated explicitly to quantum technologies.56

## **5.1. Foundational Standards: Terminology and Hardware Benchmarking**

Mirroring the ISO/IEC approach to initial semantic alignment, the IEEE’s foundational work focuses on bridging the communication gap within the emergent quantum industry, but with a specific focus on hardware benchmarking and performance metrics.

| Project Number | Title | Focus Area and Market Context |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **IEEE P7130** | Standard for Quantum Technologies Definitions | Aims to define terms explicitly related to the physics of quantum computing (e.g., superposition, quantum tunneling, entanglement) to build rigid consensus and reduce fragmentation among multidisciplinary stakeholders.56 |
| **IEEE P7131** | Standard for Quantum Computing Performance Metrics & Performance Benchmarking | Standardizes performance metrics for hardware and software, providing an objective, mathematical framework to benchmark the effectiveness, error rates, and execution quality of wildly diverse quantum architectures.6 |

The development of **IEEE P7131** addresses a critical, ongoing market failure within the quantum industry. As of 2022, various competing hardware manufacturers aggressively promoted proprietary performance metrics (such as IBM's "Quantum Volume" or IonQ's "Algorithmic Qubits"). This marketing fragmentation made it mathematically impossible for enterprise consumers or investors to directly and fairly compare a superconducting quantum computer against a trapped-ion system.47 By establishing standardized, scientifically rigorous performance benchmarks, the IEEE actively facilitates an objective, vendor-neutral marketplace, which is absolutely essential for guiding future capital investment and hardware procurement.

| **IEEE P2995** | Trial-Use Standard for a Quantum Algorithm Design and Development | Establishes formal guidelines and structural practices for coding, designing, and validating quantum algorithms, aiming to bring classical software engineering rigor to quantum programming.56 |
| **IEEE P3120** | Standard for Quantum Computing Architecture | Defines the rigid architectural layers and standard physical interfaces required to tightly integrate cryogenically cooled Quantum Processing Units (QPUs) with classical host computing systems.56 |
| **IEEE P3155** | Standard for Programmable Quantum Simulator | Establishes stringent mathematical specifications for quantum simulators, ensuring that classical software can accurately emulate quantum states and noise models for testing purposes prior to execution on highly expensive real hardware.56 |
| **IEEE P3172** | Recommended Practice for Post-Quantum Cryptography Migration | Provides a highly practical, enterprise-level operational framework and best practices for IT departments transitioning legacy cryptographic systems to post-quantum standards.56 |

## **5.3. IEEE's Bottom-Up Approach to Quantum Engineering**

A key, overarching insight into the IEEE's standardization methodology is its profound focus on the complete computing stack—from the bare-metal processor architecture (P3120) ascending up to algorithm design (P2995) and performance simulation (P3155). Unlike the ITU-T, which treats quantum technology predominantly as a macroscopic, black-box network asset, the IEEE views quantum computing as an advanced, highly intricate hardware engineering challenge that requires rigorous low-level integration standards.

Furthermore, IEEE P1943's approach to Post-Quantum Network Security highlights a deep understanding of practical network physics. PQC algorithms inherently require significantly larger public key sizes and ciphertext payloads than classical algorithms. IEEE P1943 emphasizes multi-layer protocol optimization to handle these massive keys, indicating a deep awareness of the practical throughput, packet fragmentation, and latency challenges that PQC integration will force upon existing switching and routing hardware.59

## **6\. Internet Research Task Force (IRTF)**

While highly active organizations like ETSI and ITU-T largely focused their massive resources on the immediate commercialization and deployment of QKD and PQC up to 2022, the Internet Research Task Force (IRTF) directed its highly specialized attention toward the long-term, visionary frontier of the industry: the true Quantum Internet. Within the IRTF, the **Quantum Internet Research Group (QIRG)** operates as the preeminent global forum for translating advanced quantum physics into practical internet routing engineering.60

## **6.1. Quantum Internet Research Group (QIRG)**

Chartered in late 2018, the QIRG deliberately leverages the IRTF’s extensive historical experience in classical internet design to address the profoundly complex, physics-defying challenges of routing delicate quantum states globally.60 The QIRG explicitly focuses on phenomena that QKD networks fundamentally cannot address, such as distributed quantum computing, quantum Byzantine agreement, and quantum-enhanced physical sensor systems.60

A true Quantum Internet fundamentally and mechanically differs from a QKDN. A QKDN uses quantum mechanics (measuring single photons) solely to establish a classical encryption key, which is then used by classical computers. In sharp contrast, the Quantum Internet relies on distributing physical quantum entanglement between distant nodes to transmit intact, unmeasured quantum states (qubits) via quantum teleportation. This requires highly experimental technologies such as quantum memories, entanglement swapping, and quantum error correction—technologies that were still largely confined to leading academic laboratories as of 2022\.60 The QIRG’s ambitious mandate is to solve the theoretical network engineering problems—such as routing optimization around low-coherence quantum memories—long before the physical hardware fully matures.60

## **6.2. Key Architectural Drafts and Use Cases**

By the end of 2022, the QIRG had fostered the development of several highly influential Internet-Drafts that lay the conceptual and mathematical groundwork for entanglement-based networking:

| Internet-Draft Name | Title | Focus Area and Technical Context |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **draft-irtf-qirg-principles** | Architectural Principles for a Quantum Internet | Establishes the foundational operating principles of quantum routing, proposing a revolutionary model where network applications specify a minimum "fidelity threshold" for entangled states, and the network dynamically utilizes quantum error correction mechanisms to deliver it.61 |
| **draft-irtf-qirg-quantum-internet-use-cases** | Application Scenarios for the Quantum Internet | Categorizes and rigorously describes specific applications (far beyond mere key distribution) that require end-to-end physical entanglement, outlining the generic but massive requirements these applications impose on the underlying network infrastructure.62 |
| **draft-irtf-qirg-qi-multiplane-arch** | A Multiplane Architecture Proposal for the Quantum Internet | Proposes a consistent, scalable reference architecture based on the strict separation of network planes (a classical control plane vs. a quantum data plane). It emphasizes agility, sustainability, and pliability, utilizing lessons learned from early QKD deployments to integrate complex quantum routing with classical internet operations.63 |

## **6.3. The Paradigm Shift from QKD to Entanglement Routing**

The highly theoretical work of the QIRG yields crucial insights into the long-term evolutionary trajectory of quantum networks. The core architectural concept introduced in draft-irtf-qirg-principles—the idea of "fidelity" as a primary network metric—represents a radical, fundamental departure from classical networking theory. In classical IP routing, packet delivery is essentially binary: the packet either arrives intact, or it is dropped and retransmitted. In a Quantum Internet, because of ambient environmental decoherence, a qubit cannot be simply copied (due to the no-cloning theorem) and may arrive in a partially degraded, noisy state.

The QIRG’s framework suggests an entirely new service model where applications request a specific probability of quantum state preservation (fidelity). The network must then route the connection through highly specialized quantum repeaters capable of performing entanglement purification and distillation to meet that strict threshold.61 This requires an incredibly complex, tightly synchronized classical control plane operating alongside the quantum data plane—a massive engineering challenge directly addressed by the multiplane architecture proposed in draft-irtf-qirg-qi-multiplane-arch.63

## **7\. Cross-Organizational Synthesis and Future Outlook**

Evaluating the global standardization landscape prior to the end of 2022 reveals a surprisingly high degree of implicit, and increasingly explicit, coordination among these historically independent organizations. Rather than engaging in destructive, overlapping format wars that have plagued other technological dawnings, the organizations largely respected distinct, complementary boundaries based on their historic domains of expertise.

1. **Vocabulary and Foundational Hardware**: ISO/IEC JTC 1 WG 14 and IEEE P7130 defined the ontological framework, ensuring that when an engineer says "qubit" or "coherence," it possesses a universally agreed-upon physical and mathematical meaning. IEEE P7131 simultaneously drove the standardization of hardware performance metrics to stabilize and rationalize the commercial quantum computing market.  
2. **Component Security Evaluation**: ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 27 adapted the classical Common Criteria to encompass the physical vulnerabilities inherent in quantum optics. This created the mandatory legal and operational framework for government procurement of quantum systems.  
3. **Applied Cryptography and PQC Migration**: ETSI took the lead in the rapid integration of Post-Quantum Cryptography and the creation of highly pragmatic, hybrid transition mechanisms (combining classical, PQC, and QKD algorithms), providing a near-term, defense-in-depth strategy against the impending CRQC threat.  
4. **Telecommunications Architecture and Interoperability**: The ITU-T utilized its deep historical experience in carrier networks to define the sprawling functional architecture, SDN control integration, signaling protocols, and QoS parameters necessary for scaling QKD from point-to-point laboratory links to global, multi-vendor telecommunications grids.  
5. **Future-Proofing the Quantum Internet**: The IRTF operated over a much longer time horizon, solving the complex mathematical routing, entanglement swapping, and fidelity metrics required for the eventual deployment of universal, entanglement-based quantum communication networks.

A critical, unifying second-order observation is the pervasive, dominant influence of Software-Defined Networking (SDN) across almost all applicable standard bodies (notably ETSI GS QKD 015/018 and ITU-T Y.3805). This broad consensus indicates that the industry acknowledges the extreme fragility and cost of quantum optical links. Managing fiber attenuation, dynamically re-routing highly sensitive keys around broken fibers, and preventing classical optical signals from drowning out single-photon transmissions requires centralized, software-based orchestration. Quantum networks, therefore, are being born inherently "cloud-native" and software-defined, entirely skipping the decentralized, hardware-routing phase that characterized the early growth of the classical internet.

## **8\. Conclusion**

As of the end of 2022, the status of global quantum technology standardization was characterized by aggressive, highly structured, and profoundly coordinated development across multiple interconnected fronts. The organizations examined—ETSI, ITU-T, ISO/IEC, IEEE, and IRTF—successfully laid the massive foundational groundwork required to transition quantum technologies out of academic physics departments and into highly reliable commercial engineering deployments.

The strategy adopted across the entire ecosystem has been markedly pragmatic and risk-aware. Instead of waiting for perfect, algorithmic post-quantum algorithms to be mathematically proven unconditionally secure over decades, or waiting for highly unstable entanglement-based quantum repeaters to mature in the laboratory, the standardization bodies moved rapidly to commercialize prepare-and-measure QKD and to legally mandate hybrid cryptographic models. Through rigorous architectural modeling, highly specific component-level security evaluation frameworks, and standardized cloud application interfaces, these organizations have successfully ensured that as quantum hardware continues to evolve at breakneck speed, the global telecommunications infrastructure is thoroughly prepared to securely integrate, control, and commercialize it.
